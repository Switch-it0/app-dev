# Drawn In: What a Spider-Verse and a Dragon Taught Me About Animation 🕷️🐉

## Why these two are my favorites

> **Out of everything I've watched, these are the two that changed how I *look* at animation.**
> They didn't just entertain me, they made me curious about how the thing was made.

*Spider-Man: Across the Spider-Verse* was the first film that made me see the frame itself. Different dimensions literally move differently, one character is animated at a slower rate than everything around him, and the colours shift depending on whose story you're in. I came out of it wanting to know how a team even *decides* something like that and that's when animation stopped being "cartoons" to me and became a craft I want to learn.

*How to Train Your Dragon* answered the other half of the question: story. Its two best scenes have almost no dialogue at all, a boy taming a dragon by drawing & a first flight and they hit harder than any speech. That taught me that a story is built out of trust, silence, and what the characters lose, not just plot beats.

Together they're the reason I'm starting to love how animation works: one shows me what the medium can *look* like, the other shows me what it can *feel* like. Somewhere between them is the kind of story I'd like to learn how to make.

## 1. Spider-Man: Across the Spider-Verse

**Released:** 2023 · **Directors:** Joaquim Dos Santos, Kemp Powers, Justin K. Thompson · **Studio:** Sony Pictures Animation
<br>*Sequel to* __Into the Spider-Verse__ *(2018)*, which won the Academy Award for Best Animated Feature.

The film is not "one animation style with guests." It is a *portfolio* of every dimension carries its own line quality, palette, and even its own sense of time. The result in a visual essay about who gets to be Spider-Man.

### The opening in Earth-65

> "Anybody can wear the mask."
>
> — the idea the whole trilogy keeps circling

The first 15 minutes are an **emotional short film** smuggled into a blockbuster: Gwen Stacy's backstory, her drum kit, her watercolor world, and the slow fracture in her relationship with her father, Captain Stacy. It is quiet where the rest of the film is loud and it earns the loud parts.

> **Why it works**
>
> > The scene withholds action until you care. By the time Gwen is thrown into a multiversal chase, you already know exactly what she is afraid of losing.
>
> That contrast is deliberate: *stillness first, spectacle second.*

### Spider-Punk's frame rate

Hobie Brown is animated **"on twos"**, roughly 12 drawings per second instead of 24, while everything around him runs smoother.

| Layer | Frame rate | Effect on screen |
| :--- | :---: | :--- |
| Background / other characters | 24 fps | Fluid, cinematic |
| Hobie Brown (Spider-Punk) | 12 fps | Jagged, hand-made, *deliberately* out of step |

That stutter is characterization: he refuses the system's tempo, so the film renders him refusing it. His collage-and-punk-zine look a like torn paper, halftone dots, screenprint misregistration that reinforces a protective, and anti-authority bond with Miles. He is the one adult who treats Miles like a peer.

### Six art styles, one movie

```text
Earth-65        → watercolor washes, mood-driven palette (Gwen)
Earth-1610      → Miles' home dimension, comic-ink + Ben-Day dots
Earth-50101     → Mumbhattan: saturated, painterly, high-key colour
Earth-928       → Nueva York / Spider Society, sleek and corporate-clean
Earth-138       → Spider-Punk: ripped-paper punk zine, 12 fps
Earth-42        → the shadow dimension: desaturated, hostile, wrong
```

The film reportedly juggles **up to six distinct art styles**, and the transitions are never decorative, each shift tells you *whose* story you are inside.

## 2. How to Train Your Dragon

**Released:** 2010 · **Directors:** Chris Sanders & Dean DeBlois · **Studio:** DreamWorks Animation
<br>**Score:** John Powell — a soundtrack people still hum fifteen years later.

Where *Across the Spider-Verse* shouts its style, *Dragon* whispers it. Its two signature sequences are both about **saying nothing at all**.

### Forbidden Friendship

- **What happens:** Hiccup sits down in the dirt and draws Toothless. Toothless draws back. A single touch, a hand on a snout and ends the war between them.
- **What it proves:** *Non-verbal storytelling* can carry more weight than any line of dialogue.
- **How it sounds:** John Powell's cue of the same name, all soft strings and a held breath.

> No dialogue. No narration. No explanation.
> Just a boy, a dragon, and a drawing in the mud. 🐾

### Test Drive

1. Hiccup builds the tail fin.
2. Toothless refuses then relents.
3. They fly. Really fly.
4. The score opens up, and the movie becomes a different movie.

**Test Drive** is the payoff to *Forbidden Friendship*: the same trust, converted into motion. Thrilling action and bond-building in the same breath — the rare set piece that is *about* a relationship rather than interrupting one.

The ending matters most: both characters **lose a piece of themselves**. Hiccup a leg, Toothless a tail fin and each is made whole by what the other is missing. Two half-creatures, one functional pair.

> ### One-line summary
> **Across the Spider-Verse** changes how the frame *looks*. **How to Train Your Dragon** changes how the silence *feels*. Watch both.
